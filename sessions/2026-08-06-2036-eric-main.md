**How this came up, and how long it took to find.** The operator created an offering spanning
May–September; it showed 9/10–9/16 only. Diagnosis was fast (owned-day mask, seed wrote seven
rows). **Explaining it was not.** He asked "what is ownership" four times across the exchange and
my first three answers all failed — they described the mechanism in terms of the model it came
from (boat-days, coexistence, partition grain) rather than what it *did*. What landed was
"blocks are a denylist, ownership is an allowlist, both run." Two corrections from him were
load-bearing: **seats are crew, reservations have guests**, and **we sell a boat for a time slot,
not guests on a boat** — the second one is what showed the gate was at the wrong grain.

**The concept was wrong, not just the ergonomics.** Ownership assumed a stretch where Muster and
Xola both sell. That never existed: Xola sells until the cutover, Muster after. It gated on an
operator chore that protected nothing.

**Dropped the table rather than deprecating in place.** The operator asked to keep it, then
reconsidered, and drop is right: an allowlist row records a permission, not an event, so there is
no history in it — and prod holds zero rows (he checked). Keeping it costs the one thing it ever
cost, which is a reader finding a table they cannot identify.

**Dropping it exposed a dead test.** `tablesInSchema()` in `integrity-coverage.test.ts` only
matched `create table`. This is the FIRST `drop table` in the repo's history, so nobody had found
that the "classifies nothing that isn't a table" test — whose stated job is catching a lingering
entry for a dropped table — could never fire. Reader now replays create/drop in filename order.
